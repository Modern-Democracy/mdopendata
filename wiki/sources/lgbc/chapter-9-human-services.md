## Source Claims

Chapter 9 defines human services as education, recreation and cultural services, public health and health services, social welfare, and housing. It states that many social welfare, health, and housing services are primarily federal or provincial, while BC local governments play important roles in education, parks and recreation, libraries, museums, public health, and social housing.

Education is presented as a large and expensive local-government-related function, but the chapter also emphasizes provincial control over standards, funding, and school-district budgets. The education section is therefore mainly a governance and responsibility-mapping source for municipal portal use.

Parks and recreation are described as one of the largest expenditure areas for many municipalities and regional districts. The chapter gives direct examples of measures for trails, indoor and outdoor recreation space, park maintenance staff, recreation courses, participant hours, and operating costs.

Libraries are described as locally funded services governed by separate library boards. The chapter identifies annual statistics collected for revenue, expenditure, collections, cardholders, programs, questions answered, circulation, service-area population, and visits.

Museums are described as smaller local-government or nonprofit-supported cultural services. The chapter lists quantitative measures for market share, sales income per visitor, self-sufficiency, cost per visitor, marketing, human resources, visitor diversity, satisfaction, publications, presentations, information requests, staff training, and volunteer hours.

Public health is described as primarily integrated into regional health authorities, with continuing municipal and regional-district roles through bylaws, planning consultation, health hazards, smoking bylaws, water fluoridation, and some local public-health functions.

Social housing is described as a welfare and affordability function involving federal, provincial, nonprofit, cooperative, local regulatory, and some direct local-government roles. Local tools include official-plan and growth-strategy provisions, zoning changes, rental conversion controls, rental standards, housing agreements, fast-tracked approvals, special-needs policies, and local housing corporations.

The chapter's summary states that human services are generally provided to people rather than property, are labour intensive, hard to measure, personally provided, and sometimes politically sensitive.

## Charlottetown Prototype Implications

Chapter 9 supports a human-services portal domain, but it should start with responsibility mapping and source-completeness review because many functions are shared with or led by non-municipal bodies.

For Charlottetown, the immediate prototype should focus on:

- Separating municipal, provincial, school-board, health-authority, nonprofit, and private roles before assigning performance responsibility.
- Treating parks and recreation as the strongest direct municipal benchmark source in the chapter.
- Treating libraries and museums as governance, funding, access, and usage domains with separate boards or nonprofit delivery arrangements.
- Treating public-health and social-housing metrics as multi-agency evidence, not municipal-only performance scores.
- Linking human-services measures to population, demographics, service areas, and eligibility criteria before comparison.

Chapter 9 is useful for broadening municipal performance beyond property-facing services, but it requires stronger caveats than Chapters 7 and 8 because responsibility and outcome attribution are more fragmented.

## Review Limits

Chapter 9 is BC-specific and reflects institutional arrangements as of 2008. Education, public health, and social housing cannot be treated as municipal-only performance domains without a jurisdiction-mapping review for Charlottetown and PEI.

The chapter provides direct measure examples for parks and recreation, libraries, museums, public health, and social housing, but it does not provide current standards, Charlottetown data availability, or public scoring criteria.

Human-service outcomes are difficult to attribute because they depend on demographics, household conditions, senior-government programs, nonprofit capacity, and individual participation.
